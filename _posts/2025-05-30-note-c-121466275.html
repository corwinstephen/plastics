I watched Jordan Peterson’s Jubilee debate last night and in the middle of it I figured it all out. I know exactly why he’s controversial. It has nothing to do with him being right or wrong.

Most people are highly reductive in their pursuit of knowledge, and they think definitions are absolute. Almost everyone in this debate started out by trying to get Peterson to define something so that they could point out scenarios in which the definition falls short, and when they do that, they think they've discredited his him. When instead of conceding, Peterson then proceeds to make his definition more robust, they roll their eyes and say that he's just leaning on vague definitions in order to make it impossible for him to be wrong. But that's not at all what's happening.

What's really happening is that Peterson understands intuitively that there are no absolutes--that language is imperfect and that everything can only be described imperfectly. When he dodges being called a Christian, it's not because he's afraid of the implications, it's because he can see that the person calling him a Christian has their own concept of what that means, and that saying, "Yes" to a naive person will be read as an absolute (you are a Christian, therefor...) when it fact it's a relative. I thought his analogy about literature later in the debate was extremely apt: Someone asked him "if Christianity is a spectrum, what percentage Christian do I need to be in order to be considered Christian", and Peterson responded, if literature is a spectrum, and we agree that a porno mag is on one end and Dostoevsky is on the other, what is the cutoff for a piece of writing to be considered great? The idea being that though there is no answer to that question, that in no way undermines the idea that there is nevertheless a distinct category called Great Literature. That we can debate what falls into that category in no way suggests that the category doesn't exist.

This is the way Peterson thinks about the world: He recognizes that though absolutes do exist, they are impossible to define with language. But that does not mean that they cannot be understood or aren't worth pursuing. What it means is that one must approach them with the understanding that definitions themselves are fluid, just like the category of great literature is fluid, and are only useful insofar as they help us to learn about and understand the absolutes intuitively.

Ultimately I think this is a bottom-up vs top-down approach to the world. Do definitions create things, or do things create definitions? What is a cloud, for example? Can you define it? Are 3 water molecules in the air a cloud? Or do there need to be more? If more, how many more? Does it need to be visible? How close together do they need to be? How long to they need to be together for? What if there are only two water molecules and one bit of carbon trapped between them? Yes, we have a discrete definition for a cloud, but that does not mean something that falls outside that definition might not also be a cloud. Something is not either a cloud or not a cloud. Rather, a cloud is a pattern. Things can be more or less cloudlike. Everything that exists is some percentage cloud.

The reductionists in this debate would ask, "Oh, so your definition of a cloud is that everyone is a cloud? Am I a cloud?" thinking that this statement sounds ridiculous and therefor disproves the whole concept. But in fact it’s true, and all it really proves is their lack of understanding of the relative world we live in.
